A realism of the subdued, and what it must repair before it can be anthologised

§ IX.AWhat the anthology is an ontology of
Art in Theory is not a history of art and not a reader in aesthetics. It is an archive of positions taken — and the editorial apparatus, the headnote before each text, exists to convert a document into a position by naming what it was against. That is the book's real ontology: an idea enters not by being true but by being legible as a refusal of something else that was current at the time. ACTUAL–VERIFIED
Three conditions follow from that, and Sub-Realism should be measured against them before anything else:
It must be positional, not descriptive. A text gets in because it takes a side. This is where Sub-Reality, as it currently stands, is weakest — it was born as an analytic category, the result of reading Mirror, and analytic categories are what the anthology's editors write, not what it anthologises. Sub-Reality describes a condition of the image; Sub-Realism must assert an obligation.
It must name its antagonist precisely. Cubism against Symbolist retreat; Constructivism against easel painting; the Critique of Originality against the myth of the authentic mark. "Anti-simulacra" is the right instinct but is currently aimed at a mood rather than at a doctrine. The nameable antagonist exists and is already in the book — see IX.B.
It must have a practice and a constituency. The anthology admits solitary theorists only when a practice formed around them. A manifesto with one signatory is a document; with nine it is a section. This is not a philosophical requirement, it is a bibliographic one, and it is the cheapest of the three to satisfy.
So the honest score: on (2) Sub-Realism is a page of work away; on (1) it needs the conversion set out in Repair 1; on (3) it needs the Berlin gathering already on your roadmap. None of the three requires a new idea. VIRTUAL–INFERRED
§ IX.BInheritance and break
The instinct to place Sub-Realism next to Surrealism is a trap, and your own terminology lock already resists it — Surrealism is one axis of comparison, not the ground. In the anthology's terms the resemblance is even weaker than in philosophical terms: Surrealism enters the book as a programme for producing images (automatism, the marvellous, psychic dictation). Sub-Realism produces nothing new by that route. It is a discipline of recording and reading. Its actual relatives sit two parts earlier and one part later.
Placement against the 1900–2000 structure
The antagonist, named
Section VIII.22 is where Sub-Realism earns its position, and the argument is one sentence long: Baudrillard and Sub-Realism agree entirely about the diagnosis and disagree about the tense. The simulacrum thesis says the referent has been liquidated — there is no longer an original behind the circulating image. The sub-realist claim is that the referent was not liquidated but impounded: it was recorded, it persists in the material as an excess over what anyone could say about it at the time, and it becomes readable from a later space-time. Hyperreality is a claim about circulation; sub-reality is a claim about the deposit. The one does not refute the other — it says the simulacrum thesis mistook the unreadable for the absent. VIRTUAL–INFERRED
That is a position, it has an addressee in the book, and it is defensible without any metaphysics beyond what Bergson already gives you. I would make it the first line of the manifesto.
Why "realism" and not "surrealism"
Set beside IV.11 the family resemblance is unmistakable: Sub-Realism is a realism, and its differentia is a time-lag. Classical realism holds that the artist reports what is the case. Critical realism holds that the artist reports what the official account conceals. Sub-Realism holds that the artist cannot know what they are reporting, because the suppressed content is not available to the artist's own language either — it enters the record as surplus, and only a later reader can lift it out. That is a genuinely new position in the sequence, and it explains the prefix better than any spatial metaphor: sub- marks not a lower layer but a deferred legibility.
A terminological consequence worth taking seriously. If the above holds, the vertical scheme dream / surrealism — memory / sub-realism should be demoted from structural principle to a historical footnote. It buys you a mnemonic and costs you the argument above, because it re-spatialises exactly the term you have committed to de-spatialising. VIRTUAL–INFERRED
§ IX.CThe part that does not exist yet
The 2003 edition closes with VIII.24, The Condition of History, running to 2000. Everything Sub-Realism responds to happens after that terminus: the networked image, the loss of the negative, generative synthesis, and the collapse of the evidentiary status of the photograph. A volume running to 2030 needs a Part IX, and the shape of it is fairly forced. VIRTUAL–INFERRED
Note what IX.26 does for you. Once images can be generated without ever having been recorded, the index stops being a boring technical property of photography and becomes the scarce thing. Sub-Realism's whole apparatus — a recording that holds more than its maker intended, a reader who arrives later to claim it — presupposes an index. Before 2015 that presupposition was invisible because it was universal. After 2015 it is a position. The concept was formulated in 2021 for reasons internal to Tarkovsky and the Soviet censor; its historical moment turned out to be something else. That is not a weakness in the argument, it is the argument's luck, and the anthology's editors would say so in the headnote.
§ IX.DFour repairs
These are the load-bearing ones. Each is stated as a problem in the current formulation, then a proposed replacement. All four are mine, not yours. VIRTUAL–INFERRED
Repair 1 — Access, not layer: three words where you currently have one
The problem. Your terminology lock states that Sub-Reality is a mode of access within virtuality, not a third ontological layer. But the thesis says sub-reality is "an opaque, partially visible, and subdued part of the actual matter captured within the film" — a part, within, of the actual. That is a layer, and it sits in the actual, which contradicts both halves of the lock. The phrase "sub-reality in Mirror" carries the contradiction in its preposition, and it will be the first thing a hostile reader picks up.
The repair. One word is doing three jobs. Split it:
Sealing — the operation. What the filmmaker does to duration, intuitively, without knowing what is being sealed. Belongs to the process of making.
The seal — the material result. An actual property of the recording: the surplus of the record over the intention and over the narrative economy that contains it. Verifiable, pointable-at, present in the film print. This is what is "in" Mirror.
Sub-reality — the mode of access under which a seal becomes readable from a later space-time. Not in the film. Not in the spectator. A relation between two dated positions, which is exactly what a "perspective within virtuality" has to be.
Then the sentence you want is: Mirror is readable sub-realistically because it bears a seal. No layer, no possession, no preposition problem. The 2021 formulation is preserved as the discovery of the seal; the lock is preserved as the definition of the access. And you gain the term the movement was missing — sealing names what an artist does, and a movement needs a verb.
Repair 2 — Lectorship needs an admissibility test, not a truth condition
The problem. If the lector reconnects the de-linked image to "life itself" and no reading is privileged, then any reading whatever qualifies, and the anti-simulacra programme dissolves — a discipline that cannot exclude anything is indistinguishable from the free circulation of meaning it opposes. This is the objection that Section VII.21 handed down and never answered.
The repair. Do not reach for a truth condition — you cannot have one, and claiming one would put you back inside the correspondence model the whole project abandons. Reach instead for admissibility, the way a court does: not "is this reading true" but "is this reading the kind of thing that can be argued with." Four tests, all checkable by a third party:
Anchored. It points to a determinate trace in the recording that exceeds the narrative economy — the documentary insert, the held duration, the thing the shot caught and the film never uses. Present or absent; no interpretation needed to check.
Doubly dated. It names two space-times, the sealed one and the reading one, and says what the difference between them makes readable. A reading that could have been produced at the time of production is criticism, not lectorship.
Counter-narrative-bearing. It identifies the specific account the seal cuts against. Ambiguity alone does not qualify; a suppression must be named, and can be wrong.
Actualised. It produces an artefact — text, film, work — that can itself be sealed and read. A reading that stays in the head has not been made.
Note what this buys: the fourth test converts your programme from a hermeneutics into a practice with a reproduction mechanism. Readings become works, works get read, and the corrective is peer contestation over time rather than correspondence to fact. That is exactly how the anthology's own successful positions propagated. Epistemic caution: this is a set of disciplinary norms, not a verification procedure in the scientific sense, and should never be described as one.
Repair 3 — "Pre-verbal": give up the chronology, keep the dependency, gain a testable claim
The problem. The semiological objection is old and unanswered: cinema is thoroughly coded — shot scale, cutting, genre, the whole learned grammar — and "pre-verbal" smuggles in an origin myth of unmediated perception. Metz's point was precisely that cinema is a language system without a langue; Eco's, that there is no code-free image. If Sub-Realism rests on cinema being chronologically or developmentally prior to language, it rests on something indefensible, and reviewers in film studies will stop reading there.
The repair. Deleuze does not actually need the chronological claim, and neither do you. His term is signaletic material: cinema's matter includes linguistic features but is not organised by double articulation — the utterable is a second-order operation on it, not its condition. So restate the claim as one about dependency, not priority: the cinematic image is not subordinated to the sayable. Coded, yes. Coded linguistically, no.
And then take the step that makes this empirical rather than philosophical, because it is the strongest move available to the whole project:
Censorship apparatuses are language-shaped. They read scripts, titles, dialogue, synopses, denunciations, and they read them reliably. They read duration, framing, the ambient content of a recorded street, and the surplus at the edge of a shot — unreliably, and always late.The empirical core of Sub-Realism, stated as a claim that could be shown false
This is checkable in archives: Goskino's script-stage interventions, the Hays Office's word lists, the PRC's synopsis registration, every regime's ratio of pre-production to post-production suppression. If the ratio is what you expect, Sub-Realism has an evidentiary base no rival position in Part IX possesses, and the pre-verbal claim becomes a consequence of an institutional fact rather than a metaphysical premise. If the ratio is not what you expect, you have learned something more valuable than the concept.
A second terminological caution. Aphasia is a clinical term for an acquired neurological language disorder. Your use is metaphorical, and the metaphor is licensed inside the argument by Mirror itself — the film opens on the treatment of a stammer and releases its title on "I can speak." That is a gift, so take it explicitly: mark the term as borrowed from the film's own opening rather than from neurology, and the objection converts into a citation.
Repair 4 — The practice: what this commits you to as an artist
The problem. Three practices currently sit under one name — conceptual work (Bu Bir Pipodur), cinematic VR at 180° and 360°, and classical cinematography (Waiting for the Past) — without a statement of what makes them one programme rather than a portfolio. And in one case the medium looks, on the face of it, to contradict the theory.
4a — Immersion is the enemy, and 360° is the answer anyway. The obvious objection first: the time-image depends on the crack, the out-of-field, the withheld. A 360° sphere gives you everything, and total givenness abolishes the out-of-field — which should make it the least sub-realist format imaginable. Presence is the opposite of deferral.
The inversion is where the interest lies. In a 360° recording, the out-of-field is not abolished but redistributed: it is no longer a property of the frame, it is a property of attention. Every viewer sees a fraction; the sphere records vastly more than any single pass can actualise; no two viewings coincide, and no viewing exhausts the record. This is, quite literally, the first image form in which the seal is a measurable quantity — recorded-but-unseen becomes an arithmetic fact about the artefact rather than an interpretive claim about it. Nobody, including the maker, can have seen the whole of what the camera sealed. That makes cinematic VR not a gadget attached to Sub-Realism but its native medium, and it gives you a formal problem worth a decade: how to compose for a viewer who is guaranteed to miss most of it, without either punishing them or pretending the missed portion does not exist.
4b — Conceptual art: stage the lector, not the paradox. The Magritte–Foucault lineage that Bu Bir Pipodur inherits ends in a paradox about representation — the image is not the thing. That paradox is complete; nothing further is extractable from it, which is why it has been restaged for fifty years. The sub-realist turn is to change the tense: not this is not a pipe, but this was a pipe, and the record of it now holds more than could be said about it then. The consequence for the work is concrete: the piece should make the reading apparatus visible — the dating, the anchor, the counter-narrative — rather than the representational paradox. A conceptual work in which the four admissibility tests of Repair 2 are the exhibited material is a better piece than one more caption denying its image.
4c — The synthetic image, and the sharpest line you have. A generated image has no seal. There is no surplus, because nothing was recorded — every part of it derives from the intention that prompted it, and there is no later space-time from which something withheld can be lifted out, because nothing was withheld. This is a technical criterion, not a moral one, and it should be stated coldly: Sub-Realism is not against synthetic images, it is the art of the seal in an age of images that have none. Say it that way and "anti-simulacra" stops being a mood and becomes a specification.

IX.27 a Mustafa Ozan Karyağdı— ‘Sub-Realism: Twelve Propositions on the Seal’
Editorial headnote
The concept of sub-reality was formulated in a master's thesis on Tarkovsky's Mirror submitted at Kadir Has University in 2021, in the course of an argument about Deleuze's lectosign and the kinds of thinking proper to filmmaking and to spectatorship. Its occasion was the Soviet censor: the thesis observed that a film made under a regime that forbade the description of its own conditions had nevertheless recorded those conditions, and that what it recorded became readable only from a later position — the author's own, in Istanbul, half a century downstream. The concept was thus first offered as an analytic category, a way of accounting for something a particular film had done.
The propositions below, written in Berlin five years later, convert that category into a position, and the conversion is the interesting part. In the interval the argument's antagonist had changed. Against the censor, sub-reality was a thesis about repression; against the generated image, it becomes a thesis about the index. The claim that a recording holds a surplus over its maker's intention is unremarkable so long as all images are recordings; it becomes contentious the moment images can be produced that were never recorded at all. Karyağdı's wager is that the critique of the referent conducted in the 1980s (see VIII.22) reached the wrong conclusion from the right evidence — that what had been described as the liquidation of the real was in fact its impoundment, and that impounded things can be recovered by a discipline of reading. Against Baudrillard, whose account he otherwise accepts almost in full, he holds that a seal is by definition openable.
The text's difficulty, which its author acknowledges in the eleventh proposition, is the one that has attended every theory of the active reader since Barthes: a reading that cannot be wrong cannot be argued with, and the four tests offered here are proposed as norms of admissibility rather than criteria of truth. Its second difficulty is the claim, inherited from Deleuze, that the cinematic image is not subordinated to the sayable — a claim the semiologists of the 1960s had reason to resist, and which Karyağdı restates as a proposition about institutions rather than about perception. The affiliation with Surrealism, which the name invites, is explicitly refused: the lineage claimed here runs through Realism as Critique (IV.11) and the reader-centred revisions of the late 1960s (VII.21), and the movement's own preferred description of itself is as a realism with a time-lag.
The text
The real was not liquidated. It was sealed. A seal is openable; this is what distinguishes our position from the one it inherits everything else from.
To seal is to record more than one intends, in a duration one did not fully compose. Every camera does it. Only some makers work for it.
The seal is actual and material. It is in the recording, and it can be pointed at. Sub-reality is not in the recording: it is the perspective from which a seal becomes readable, and a perspective requires two dates.
Therefore no work contains sub-reality, and no artist produces it. Works bear seals; sub-reality is how they are read, later, by someone whose present differs from the sealed present in a way that can be stated.
What is unsayable is not what language lacks words for. It is what an apparatus is organised to catch. Apparatuses of suppression are built out of language — they read scripts, they read titles, they read denunciations. They read duration badly and they read it late.
This is why the audiovisual holds what the sentence cannot. Not because the image is innocent or prior or unmediated — it is none of these — but because it is not subordinated to the sayable, and the machinery that polices the sayable therefore passes over it.
The lector is not an interpreter. An interpreter recovers a meaning that was placed; a lector claims a surplus that was not placed by anyone, and the claim is made from a dated position that must be declared.
A reading is admissible when it is anchored in a determinate trace, doubly dated, bearing a named counter-narrative, and actualised as a work. These are norms, not proofs. We do not have proofs and will not pretend to.
The fourth norm is the movement's engine: a reading that does not become a work has not been made. Works are sealed in their turn. This is how the practice reproduces itself and how it corrects itself, which is the only correction available to us.
A generated image bears no seal. Nothing was withheld from it because nothing was recorded, and there is no later position from which anything can be lifted out. We are not opposed to such images. We are the art of the seal in an age that no longer produces one by default.
The spherical recording is our native form, for the reason that looks at first like a refutation: it gives everything, and so no viewer can receive it. What was framing becomes attention. What was withheld becomes unvisited. The seal is, for the first time, a quantity.
We do not ask what a work means. We ask what it caught, who was prevented from saying it, and what has changed since — and then we are obliged to make something with the answer.
